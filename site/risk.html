⚠️ Risk Analysis · Kill Switches · Red Team Findings

We've stress-tested the model. Here's what could break it.

HomeGuarantee operates at the intersection of public housing systems, private landlords, vulnerable tenants, and a Works employment program. We've identified 10 kill switches, 5 structural tensions, and 7 red-team attacks on our core assumptions. Kill switches are automatic pause triggers — not aspirational commitments.

Kill Switches

10 automatic pause and pivot triggers

🛑
Kill switches are not advisory. Breaching a kill switch threshold triggers automatic enrollment pause and a mandatory board-level review before resuming. They are wired into the financial model and operating procedures.

25% claim rate on $50/unit/mo fee = $150/unit/year expected loss vs $600/unit/year revenue. At 25%, Stabilization is net-negative even before operating costs. Model viability collapses.
Structural Tensions

Inherent trade-offs that can't be fully resolved

Tension 1

Mission scale vs. Financial discipline

The more vulnerable tenants we house, the higher the expected claim rate. Maximizing mission impact (serving the highest-need tenants) creates the worst unit economics. Stabilization tier is the starkest version: lowest-cost tenants, highest claim rate, 16% margin.

Resolution: Cross-subsidize. High-margin Basic and Managed tiers fund the below-cost Stabilization mission. Do not optimize mission and profit from the same unit.
Tension 2

Works employment income vs. Tenant SSI stability

Works employment creates earned income that reduces SSI. Stabilization tenants depend on SSI-calculated rent. If Works employees live in Stabilization units they renovated, their earned income raises their rent obligation while reducing SSI — a double-whammy. Co-location is structurally forbidden.

Resolution: Strict separation. Works transitional units ≠ Stabilization units. Two separate housing pools, two separate tracking systems. K9 validates separation at Month 3.
Tension 3

CoC partnership dependency vs. Platform independence

The Way Home CoC controls HMIS — the data backbone for tenant matching. Without CoC buy-in, the platform is landlord-side only. But full CoC dependency creates a single-partner risk: one leadership change at CFTH could halt operations.

Resolution: Multi-CoC strategy. Onboard 3 CoC partners by Month 12. Build proprietary tenant match layer as HMIS backup. K3 triggers manual fallback.
Tension 4

Distressed owner conversion speed vs. Renovation quality

Phase 2.5b depends on fast owner conversion (30-day target from HCAD outreach to signed lease). But renovation quality — the core exchange mechanism — requires 6–10 weeks per unit for Works crews. Speed and quality are in tension during the pilot ramp.

Resolution: Owners sign lease first; Works crew scheduled within 30 days of signing. Tenant placement only after habitability sign-off. No "fast move-in" shortcuts on safety.
Tension 5 · Texas SB 267

Legal protection absence vs. Market-based workaround

Texas SB 267 explicitly legalizes source-of-income discrimination. HomeGuarantee's entire business model is a workaround for this law. This creates a political risk: any future repeal of SB 267 (which is actually a positive social outcome) would reduce our market differentiation and the urgency of our guarantee product. The business model is built on a policy failure.

Response: This is an acceptable risk. SB 267 repeal would be net-positive for tenants and for the city. HomeGuarantee's moat shifts from "filling a legal gap" to "deepest landlord trust network in Houston" — which is durable regardless of regulatory change. We do not depend on the problem persisting. We depend on trust.
Red Team

Hardest attacks on our core assumptions

Attack 1: "Your $3,500 average claim is based on Oregon data. Houston landlords have older housing stock — claims will be $6,000+."
Houston housing stock is older (median build year 1978 vs Oregon 1989) and more susceptible to water damage in flood events. Our $3,500 avg payout assumption is unvalidated for Houston.
⚠️ REAL RISK. Mitigation: Conduct 12 landlord damage interviews before Month 3. Set $4,500 payout avg in stress model. K1 trigger at 18% reduces exposure if claims are higher.
Attack 2: "Small landlords won't pay $200/month for Managed tier when they can hire a local PM for $100–150/month."
Traditional PM fees in Houston are 8–12% of rent ($116–174/mo on a $1,450/mo unit). Our $200 is at the top of market — the premium requires the guarantee to clearly justify the differential.
❌ LEGITIMATE THREAT. Our differentiation must be the guarantee + Works crew expertise, not just price. If Marcus can hire a PM for $120/mo and separately buy damage insurance, we're not competitive. Response: Bundle 3-month vacancy waiver prominently; show total cost of a claim without HomeGuarantee.
Attack 3: "The Way Home CoC has 100+ partners. Why would they trust a startup with their HMIS data?"
HMIS data access requires CoC board approval and typically takes 9–18 months. CFTH (Coalition for the Homeless) has been burned by tech vendors before. API access is not granted based on a presentation.
⚠️ PROCESS RISK, NOT FATAL. Mitigation: Hire a former CFTH or HHA insider as pilot Director. Start with read-only HMIS access (lower bar). Offer data sharing reciprocity — HomeGuarantee sends lease outcome data back to HMIS. K3 is the fallback.
Attack 4: "Stabilization tenants at $290/month can't afford any unexpected cost. One broken pipe triggers default."
At 30% of $967 SSI = $290.10/mo rent. After rent, tenants have $677/mo for food, medicine, transportation, and all incidentals. Any unexpected $300 expense — broken appliance, medical co-pay, transit card — creates a housing instability cascade.
❌ TRUE STRUCTURAL VULNERABILITY. Our 18% Stabilization claim rate assumption may still be too low. The Works renovation must include appliance replacement, not just structural habitability. We must also connect tenants to emergency utility assistance programs. Stabilization unit leases should include utility caps. This is solvable but requires active services wrapping, not just a guarantee product.
Attack 5: "You have 3,280 homeless people in Houston but need landlords who own 3,280 units. That's the actual bottleneck — not demand."
True. Houston has 105,840 actionable vacant units (32× the homeless population), but they need landlord owners willing to participate. We cannot compel participation. Our tool is incentive, not mandate.
✓ ACKNOWLEDGED AND CORRECT. This is the core problem statement, not a flaw in our model. The vacancy surplus (32×) means supply is not the bottleneck — landlord trust is. That's exactly what the guarantee addresses. The 32× surplus is our most powerful investor talking point.
Attack 6: "If the program works, HUD will absorb it. Why build a company instead of a program?"
HUD has a history of piloting market-based tools and then either abandoning them (YES program scalability failures) or converting them to grant-dependent programs that lose their market discipline.
✓ THIS IS FEATURE NOT BUG. HomeGuarantee being private-market means it can operate in Texas without state or federal approval. SB 267 cannot stop a private guarantee. Speed-to-market and political independence are competitive advantages, not risks.
Attack 7: "You need $2M in guarantee fund capital upfront. That's a large ask before you've proved the model."
The guarantee fund must be pre-positioned — it's not revenue-generated. Without it, we can't credibly offer the guarantee. It must be raised before first enrollment, not as operating capital.
⚠️ FUNDRAISING RISK. Mitigation: Structure initial guarantee fund as a foundation program-related investment (PRI) rather than equity or grant. MacArthur, Ford, and JPMorgan all have PRI facilities for exactly this use case. PRI lenders receive repayment at low interest — this is capital-efficient and precedented.
