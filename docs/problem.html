Root Cause

Six barriers keeping landlords from saying yes

The Way Home's infrastructure is world-class. The remaining bottleneck is specific, diagnosable, and solvable.

1

Legal source-of-income discrimination

Texas SB 267 (2015) explicitly prohibits cities from passing ordinances that protect voucher holders. Landlords in Houston can legally turn away anyone with a Housing Choice Voucher. This is not a gap in enforcement — it is codified law. HomeGuarantee cannot change the law, but it makes voucher discrimination financially irrational.

2

Administrative friction with HUD

Landlords must schedule and pass HUD UPCS inspections, navigate complex paperwork, and wait for government payment processing — often 30–60 days after a lease starts. Many small landlords find the process more trouble than it's worth when market tenants are available. HomeGuarantee absorbs all of this friction.

3

Fear of property damage and non-payment

Perceived (not always actual) risk that formerly homeless tenants will damage property or miss rent. Research shows voucher holders are not systematically worse tenants than other low-income renters — but perception drives behavior. Oregon's OHCS data: average claim $3,500; claims rate ~12%. Insurable at scale.

4

Stigma and screening barriers

Criminal histories, prior evictions, and poor credit are common among formerly homeless individuals. Standard tenant screening automatically rejects them — before any human judgment is applied. The guarantee removes the financial stakes that make this automated screening feel necessary.

5

Affordability gap at the FMR ceiling

Houston's FMR for a 1BR is $1,241. Median asking rent is ~$1,450 — a 17% gap. Even with a voucher covering FMR, landlords expect tenants to cover the remaining $200, which many cannot. This is the smallest affordability gap of any major US metro — meaning Houston is the easiest place to test a gap-filling solution.

6

Supply gap: distressed and abandoned units locked by renovation cost NEW · Phase 2.5b

Houston's 105,840 actionable vacant units include a large segment of distressed, substandard, or abandoned properties whose owners cannot afford to bring them to HUD habitability standards. These units sit permanently off-market — costing owners $325–540/month in taxes, insurance, fines, and vandalism — while demand goes unmet. The Works Renovation Exchange converts this hidden inventory by making renovation a shared benefit, not a solo owner cost.
