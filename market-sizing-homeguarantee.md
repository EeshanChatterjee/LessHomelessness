# HomeGuarantee — Market Sizing
**TAM / SAM / SOM across Houston, Texas, United States, and Canada**
*Date: June 6, 2026*

---

## Market Definition

**Problem space:** Private-market landlords refuse to rent to formerly homeless individuals — even when those individuals have Housing Choice Vouchers, active case management, and coordinated entry support. HomeGuarantee removes that refusal by providing a financial guarantee (up to $20K/tenancy) and administrative lift that makes landlord participation low-risk and low-friction.

**Customer:** Housing authorities and continuum-of-care lead agencies pay a per-unit platform fee ($75–100/unit/month, average $87.50) to enroll active placements. Landlords are users; formerly homeless individuals are beneficiaries.

**Revenue unit:** One active enrolled unit = one formerly homeless individual placed and in tenancy, with an active guarantee. Average enrollment duration: 18–24 months.

**Annual revenue per unit:** $87.50/month × 12 = **$1,050/year**

**Constraints and scoping decisions:**
- Viable markets require a vacancy surplus (eliminates LA, NYC in Phase 1–2 per Experiment D)
- Must have active coordinated entry / HMIS infrastructure
- US market sized on HCV-adjacent population; Canada market sized on equivalent rent supplement programs but flagged as a 3–5 year adaptation (no direct HCV equivalent)
- TAM is sized on platform fee revenue; insurance margin (Phase 3) treated separately

## Houston

### Top-Down Estimate

Houston has 3,280 people experiencing homelessness (2024 PIT). Of these:
- ~60% are in The Way Home's coordinated entry system with active case management = ~1,970
- ~70% of those need a guarantee to be placed (the hardest-to-house chronic population = the 37% who remain after Houston's 63% reduction) = ~1,380 units
- Rounding to a working estimate: **1,500 enrollable units at steady state**

Revenue: 1,500 × $1,050 = **$1.575M/year**

Voucher-side check: Houston has 23,900 active HCVs (HHA + HCHA). If 25% go unused due to landlord refusal (consistent with national research), that's ~6,000 unused vouchers. HomeGuarantee's focus is the homeless-specific slice of those: ~30% = 1,800 units. Consistent with the 1,500 estimate above.

### Bottom-Up Estimate

- The Way Home: ~15,000 unique individuals served per year; placement rate ~35% annually = ~5,250 new placements/year
- HomeGuarantee's role: ~30% of placements need a guarantee (others place through existing landlord relationships) = 1,575 new enrollments/year
- Average enrollment duration: 18 months → active units at any time = 1,575 × 1.5 = **2,363 active units** (upper bound)
- At $1,050/year: **$2.48M** (upper bound)
- Conservative at 60% of upper bound: **$1.5M/year**

### Houston Market Summary

| | Current | 2–3 Year Projection |
|---|---|---|
| **TAM** | $1.6–2.5M/year | $2.0–3.0M (growing with homeless count and new vouchers) |
| **SAM** | $1.3–2.0M (85% of TAM; full coordinated entry coverage) | $1.7–2.5M |
| **SOM** | Year 1: $525K (500 units pilot) | Year 3: $1.575M (1,500 units) |

**Confidence: High.** Houston's homeless population is small, well-documented, and served by a single coordinated entry system. The unit count is bounded and verifiable.

## Texas

### Top-Down Estimate

Texas statewide PIT count (2023): **27,377** homeless across all CoCs. Applying the national 12% YoY increase trend: estimated **~30,000** in 2024.

Major Texas CoCs and estimated homeless populations:
| CoC | Estimated 2024 |
|-----|---------------|
| Houston / Harris County | 3,280 |
| Dallas / Fort Worth | ~7,000 |
| Austin / Travis County | ~4,500 |
| San Antonio / Bexar County | ~3,500 |
| Texas Balance of State | ~10,081 |
| Other CoCs (El Paso, Lubbock, etc.) | ~1,600 |
| **Total** | **~30,000** |

Addressable units: 30,000 × 60% (coordinated entry) × 30% (need guarantee) = **5,400 units**

Revenue: 5,400 × $1,050 = **$5.67M/year**

Texas HCV vouchers: Texas is ~8% of the US population and roughly 8% of the 2.4M national HCV pool = ~192,000–250,000 HCVs statewide. At 25% unused due to landlord refusal = 48,000–62,000 unused. HomeGuarantee's formerly homeless slice (~10%): 4,800–6,200 units. Consistent with the 5,400 top-down estimate.

### Bottom-Up Estimate

- Texas CoCs collectively serve ~61,000 unique individuals per year (THDSN 2023 data)
- New housing placements: ~35% of served population = 21,350/year
- HomeGuarantee's role (30% needing guarantee): ~6,400 new enrollments/year
- Active units at 18-month tenure: 6,400 × 1.5 = 9,600 (upper bound)
- At $1,050/year: **$10.1M** upper bound; conservative at 55%: **$5.5M/year**

### Texas Market Summary

| | Current | 2–3 Year Projection |
|---|---|---|
| **TAM** | $5.5–10M/year | $7–13M (growing with homeless population) |
| **SAM** | $3.9–7M (70% of TAM; markets with coordinated entry: Houston, Dallas, Austin, San Antonio) | $5–9M |
| **SOM** | Year 1: $525K (Houston only) | Year 3: $2.6M (Houston + Dallas + Austin expansion) |

**Confidence: Medium.** Texas CoC data is reasonably complete but the Balance of State CoC covers a vast and heterogeneous geography where coordinated entry is thinner.

## United States

### Top-Down Estimate

US homeless population: **771,500** (2024 HUD PIT — record high, up 18% from 2023).

**Viable market filter (Experiment D results):**
Remove LA (71,201) and NYC (140,134) as Phase 1–2 markets due to critically thin vacancy ratios (1.4×). Viable US market: **560,165 homeless in 8 of 8 other major metros analyzed, plus all other US cities.**

Addressable unit calculation:
| Step | Figure |
|------|--------|
| Viable-market homeless | 560,000 |
| % in coordinated entry with case management | 60% = 336,000 |
| % requiring a guarantee (hardest-to-house) | 30% = 100,800 units |
| Annual revenue at $1,050/unit | **$105.8M/year** |

HCV cross-check: 2.4M active HCVs nationally (HUD, 2024). Nationally, 20–25% go unused due to landlord refusal = 480,000–600,000 unused vouchers. HomeGuarantee's homeless-specific target (formerly homeless individuals with vouchers): ~15% of unused vouchers = 72,000–90,000 units. In the same range as the top-down estimate of 100,800 (top-down includes some without vouchers who need direct subsidy).

**Platform TAM: $100–120M/year** (midpoint $108M)

Insurance margin (Phase 3 additive): At $20/unit/month reinsurance premium with 10% margin on 100,800 units: $24M × 10% = **$2.4M/year** additional. Modest, included in total.

**US Total TAM: ~$110M/year**

### Bottom-Up Estimate

- US homeless service system: ~1.2M unique individuals served/year (sheltered + unsheltered)
- Housing placement exits from shelter/transitional programs: ~35% = 420,000/year
- HomeGuarantee's role (landlord-blocked placements): ~20% of all placements = 84,000 new enrollments/year
- Active units at 18-month tenure: 84,000 × 1.5 = 126,000 (upper bound)
- At $1,050/year: $132M (upper bound); conservative at 80%: **$105M/year**

Both approaches converge at ~$105–110M. High confidence in the range.

### US SAM

HomeGuarantee can't serve all viable markets immediately. SAM constraints:
- Government procurement: only markets where a housing authority has activated = reduces to ~60% of TAM in 3 years
- Coordinated entry maturity: markets with functioning HMIS and coordinated entry = ~75% of viable market
- Phase 1–3 city capacity: by Year 5, realistically 15–20 cities active

SAM = 60% of TAM = **$63–70M/year**

### US SOM

| Year | Active Units | ARR |
|------|-------------|-----|
| Year 1 | 500 (Houston pilot) | $525K |
| Year 2 | 2,000 (Houston + Texas) | $2.1M |
| Year 3 | 6,000 (Houston + Texas + Chicago + Denver + Phoenix) | $6.3M |

**SOM Year 3: ~$6.3M ARR = 6% of SAM**

Comparable SaaS benchmarks for B2G (business-to-government) platforms in housing/social services suggest 3–7% market penetration in years 1–3 is achievable for a well-funded product with a design partner city.

### US Market Summary

| | Current | 2–3 Year Projection |
|---|---|---|
| **TAM** | $105–120M/year | $130–160M (10% annual market growth) |
| **SAM** | $63–70M/year | $78–88M |
| **SOM** | $525K (Year 1) | $6.3M (Year 3) |

**Confidence: Medium-High.** The HCV program data and PIT counts are well-sourced. The 30% "needs guarantee" assumption is the key variable — if it's 20%, TAM drops to ~$70M; if 40%, it rises to ~$140M. Oregon and Seattle program data (when available) will sharpen this estimate.

## Canada

### Structural Difference — Important Caveat

Canada does not have a direct equivalent to the US Housing Choice Voucher program. Housing assistance in Canada is:
- **Fragmented by province/territory**: Each province runs its own rent supplement program, co-funded by the Canada Housing Benefit (federal contribution: $325M/year; projected to reach 300,000 households by 2027–28)
- **Lower per-unit values**: Canadian rent supplements are typically smaller than US HCV vouchers; market rents vary widely (Vancouver/Toronto = extremely expensive; Prairie cities = much more affordable)
- **No national coordinated entry standard**: Canada's Reaching Home strategy funds coordinated community plans but implementation varies significantly by city
- **Different landlord dynamic**: Source-of-income discrimination protections are stronger in many Canadian provinces (unlike Texas SB 267) — which partially reduces the landlord refusal problem, but does not eliminate it

HomeGuarantee entering Canada requires product adaptation, separate regulatory compliance (province-by-province), and a different buyer (provincial housing ministries rather than US-style PHAs). This is a **3–5 year market**, not a near-term target.

### Top-Down Estimate (Speculative)

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| PIT homeless population | 60,000 (2024) |
| Annual shelter users | 118,000 |
| % in coordinated entry needing guarantee | 60% × 25% = 15,000 units |
| Price point adjustment (65% of US = CAD ~$683/unit/year) | — |
| **Canada TAM (CAD)** | **~$10.2M CAD/year** |
| **Canada TAM (USD at 0.73)** | **~$7.5M USD/year** |

Price is lower than the US because Canadian rent supplements have lower dollar values and government budgets for program administration are smaller.

Mature markets for Canada entry (coordinated entry exists, landlord refusal documented):
- Greater Toronto Area, Vancouver / Metro Vancouver, Calgary, Edmonton, Ottawa

**Canada SAM (USD):** ~60% of TAM = **$4.5M USD/year**

**Canada SOM Year 1–3:** $0 — Canada requires product adaptation, provincial licensing, and a new partnership equivalent to CFTH. It's a strategic option to map, not a near-term operating target.

### Canada Market Summary

| | 3–5 Year Estimate |
|---|---|
| **TAM** | ~$7.5M USD/year |
| **SAM** | ~$4.5M USD/year |
| **SOM** | Year 5+ |

**Confidence: Low.** Canada's fragmented provincial structure makes this estimate highly sensitive to which provinces adopt the model and at what price point. Validate with a single provincial partner (BC or Ontario) before sizing seriously.

## Growth Drivers and Trends

**Market expanding:**
- US homelessness up 18% in 2024, 47% since 2015 — the problem is growing faster than the solutions
- HUD added 3,000 new HCVs in FY2024; expansion legislation periodically proposed (voucher-for-all bills)
- Landlord refusal worsening as rent inflation widens the FMR gap in most metros
- Federal interest in landlord incentive programs is bipartisan — USICH has active guidance; HUD CoCBuilds NOFO ($175M) funds this type of work
- Insurance market for housing-related guarantees is maturing (Oregon program operational since 2021; data accumulating)

**Market contracting risks:**
- Federal HCV funding cuts (already underway in some FY2025 proposals) could shrink the voucher-backed market
- Homelessness itself could decline if Housing First policies scale (which would reduce the market size, but validate the model)
- Source-of-income discrimination bans (if Texas reverses SB 267) would partially eliminate the barrier and reduce demand for HomeGuarantee

**Adjacent markets (not in TAM):**
- Low-income renters without homeless history who still face screening barriers: ~10× larger population, lower willingness-to-pay from housing authorities, different product
- Workforce housing / employer-sponsored housing guarantee: different buyer, different underwriting
- Refugee resettlement housing: ~100,000 arrivals/year in the US needing initial housing placement

## Key Assumptions and Risks

| # | Assumption | Confidence | Validation method |
|---|-----------|------------|------------------|
| 1 | 30% of coordinated entry placements require a guarantee | Medium | Oregon / CFTH placement data; Year 1 pilot claims |
| 2 | $87.50/unit/month is achievable from housing authorities | Medium | HHA procurement conversations (Month 6) |
| 3 | Average enrollment duration = 18 months | Medium | The Way Home exits data (90% retained 2+ years suggests 24+ month duration may be more accurate — upside) |
| 4 | LA and NYC remain non-viable through Year 3 | High | Vacancy data; would only change with major policy shift (vacancy tax) |
| 5 | Canada at 65% US price point | Low | No Canadian pilot data; highly speculative |
| 6 | 10% annual market growth | Medium | Consistent with 5-year trend; could accelerate or reverse |
| 7 | 60% of homeless are in coordinated entry | Medium | HMIS enrollment rates vary by city (Houston ~75%, rural CoCs ~30%) |
| 8 | HomeGuarantee can reach 6% SAM penetration by Year 3 | Medium | Comparable to other B2G SaaS adoption curves |

**If assumption 1 changes:**
- 20% needing guarantee → US TAM drops to ~$70M
- 40% needing guarantee → US TAM rises to ~$140M

**If assumption 2 fails (housing authorities won't pay):**
- Revenue model shifts to philanthropic/grant funding at similar rates
- TAM does not change; revenue mix changes; growth pace slows significantly
